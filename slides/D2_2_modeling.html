Silver → gold

Clean rows become answers: the gold layer

Modeling is the design of the gold layer: facts at a stated grain, dimensions around them. dbt (next session) is how we build it; this session is what to build. The map marks dbt because the models we design here become its marts.
The problem and value · Shared sales definitions

Clean records still need a sales definition

“How much did we sell?” leaves several decisions open.

  • Do cancelled orders count?
  • Does sales include delivery fees?
  • Does “by city” mean customer city or store city?
  • If a customer moves, should past sales move too?
Why this matters: A model makes these choices explicit so analysts can reproduce the same answer.
For the worked example, product revenue means delivered line net amounts. Delivery fees remain a separate measure. Use store city, as in the lab business questions. The same model may support multiple metrics, but each needs a definition.
The philosophy

Dimensional modeling gives each row a clear meaning

Organize data around the business process people want to analyze.

  • Grain: state exactly what one row represents.
  • Facts: store measurements at that grain.
  • Dimensions: provide the context used to filter and group those measurements.

For Qafila: an order line holds quantity and net amount. Product and store dimensions explain what sold and where.

Dimensional modeling is a design approach. It does not require a particular software tool. Establish the process and grain before choosing measures. The star schema on the next slide shows how the design works. Then trace the actual source rows into it.
How it works

The star schema connects facts to context

  • Date: weekday, month, weekend.
  • Product: name, category, brand.
  • Store: store name and city.
  • Customer: analytical attributes such as city.

Each fact key must match exactly one dimension row.

Use the existing diagram to trace one line through date, product, store, and customer. In this lesson the dimension keys are unique. An SCD2 dimension contains multiple versions per business key, so facts must identify a particular version. The customer analytical dimension omits direct identifiers such as mobile and national ID.

Step 1 · Define the business process

The business question determines the calculation

How much product revenue came from Riyadh stores?

  1. Find Riyadh in raw.stores.city.
  2. Match store_id to delivered raw.orders.
  3. Match order_id to raw.order_items.
  4. Add quantity × unit_price − discount.

Order 5000026: 33.46 + 2 × 121.20 = 275.86 SAR

This is the same join path and store-city definition used in Lab 2.2. The 275.86 SAR result covers the selected Riyadh order only, not all Riyadh sales. Customer city and store city are different attributes even when they happen to agree for these rows.

Ask learners to finish: one row per what? “Sales data” and “one transaction” are often too vague. Two lines may contain the same product, so order_id plus product_id is not necessarily unique. Choose order-line grain to retain detail for product analysis.

An allocation rule can distribute an order fee over lines, for example proportionally to line value. That is an explicit business decision requiring checks that allocated fees sum to the original fee. Copying the full fee onto each line does not allocate it.

Three fact types

Fact types describe different kinds of rows

Fact typeOne row representsWhat happens over time
TransactionOne sale, payment, or refund eventNew events add rows
Periodic snapshotOne product in one store on one dateEach reporting period adds observations
Accumulating snapshotOne order moving through fulfillmentThe row gains milestone timestamps
Do not infer additivity solely from fact-table type. Transaction amounts may be additive across disjoint events. Snapshot balances are often semi-additive. Accumulating facts can contain counts, durations, and amounts with different valid aggregations.

SELECT SUM(f.net_amount + o.delivery_fee)
FROM star.fct_order_items f
JOIN wh.raw.orders o USING (order_id)
WHERE f.order_id IN (5000026, 5000422);
The difference is exactly 9 SAR. Both selected orders are delivered. SUM(DISTINCT delivery_fee) is demonstrably wrong here as well: both orders legitimately have the same 9 SAR fee, so DISTINCT would retain only 9 rather than 18.
The fan-out trap · Fix the grain

A shared grain makes the calculation safe

WITH item_totals AS (
  SELECT order_id, SUM(net_amount) AS items_amount
  FROM star.fct_order_items
  GROUP BY order_id
)
SELECT o.order_id, i.items_amount, o.delivery_fee,
       i.items_amount + o.delivery_fee AS total
FROM wh.raw.orders o
LEFT JOIN item_totals i USING (order_id)
WHERE o.order_id IN (5000026, 5000422);

Both selected orders have lines. Their totals add to 327.51 SAR. For a broader report decide explicitly how to treat an order without valid lines. The purpose of this example is to make both sides of the join one row per order, exactly as the supplied dbt fct_orders model does with its lines CTE.
Before trusting the model

A reconciliation checks rows as well as money

CheckExpected in our exampleWhat a mismatch can reveal
Fact row count3 source lines = 3 fact linesA dropped row or multiplying join
Fact key uniquenessNo repeated (order_id, line_no)A grain violation
Line net total309.51 SAR in source and factA filter, calculation, or join difference
Rows assigned to UnknownExplain and monitor themMissing source references
Why this matters: Use the same time range, status rules, and quantity rules on both sides of the comparison.
Counts and totals are complementary: equal counts can hide one dropped and one duplicated row, while equal totals can hide offsetting errors. Lab 2.2 retains all quantities for the modeling exercise. The dbt mart filters quantity > 0 and quarantines non-positive quantities. Reconcile that curated mart against the correspondingly filtered source, not all raw rows.
Using the star for another question

The date dimension supports daily comparisons

WITH daily AS (
  SELECT d.date_day, d.is_weekend, s.city,
         SUM(f.net_amount) AS revenue
  FROM star.fct_order_items f
  JOIN star.dim_date d USING (date_key)
  JOIN star.dim_store s USING (store_key)
  WHERE f.order_status = 'delivered'
  GROUP BY d.date_day, d.is_weekend, s.city
)
SELECT city, is_weekend, AVG(revenue) AS avg_daily_revenue
FROM daily GROUP BY city, is_weekend;
Why this matters: First total each day. Then average those daily totals, so days with more order lines do not get extra weight.
The lab defines Friday and Saturday as the weekend and flags September 23 for its National Day comparison. The query averages observed sales days. If a city has zero-sales days, build a complete calendar-by-city set and fill those daily totals with zero before averaging. Lab dim_store city is store city.
Other choices

Other modeling styles change the tradeoffs

StyleHow it organizes dataTradeoff
StarFacts connect to descriptive dimensionsReusable analytical structure, requires grain discipline
SnowflakeSome dimensions refer to other dimensionsLess repeated hierarchy data, more joins
One big tablePrejoin measures and descriptionsConvenient for a workload, repeats attributes
Data VaultSeparate business keys, relationships, and historyIntegration and traceability, often needs serving marts

The labs use a star. The optional stretch compares it with a wide table.

These are modeling patterns, not a ranking by speed. An OBT can sit on a star for a particular consumer. Historical attribute changes require an explicit refresh or versioning strategy. Data Vault often supports integration upstream of dimensional marts.
Checkpoint

An order has three lines and one 9 SAR fee. What happens if we sum that fee after joining to the lines?

The fee contributes 9 SAR
The fee contributes 27 SAR
The fee disappears
B. The join produces three matching rows. Summing the repeated fee counts 9 SAR three times. Aggregate to order grain before combining measures.
